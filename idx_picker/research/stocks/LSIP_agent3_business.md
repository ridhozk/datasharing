## PRIORITY 1 — TREE AGE PROFILE (the central finding)

### What is actually disclosed

**FACT (AR2025 Operational Highlights, p.7).** Nucleus planted area, hectares:

| | 2021 | 2022 | 2023 | 2024 | 2025 | 1H26 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Total planted (nucleus)** | 114,111 | 111,240 | 111,940 | 111,367 | 110,982 | 109,258 |
| **Oil palm** | 93,853 | 91,151 | 91,759 | 91,152 | 90,798 | 89,559 |
| — Mature | 85,630 | 83,742 | 85,198 | 84,941 | 84,744 | n/d |
| — Immature | 8,223 | 7,409 | 6,561 | 6,211 | 6,054 | n/d |
| **Rubber** | 16,228 | 16,074 | 16,238 | 16,231 | 16,203 | 15,717 |
| **Other (cocoa, tea)** | 4,030 | 4,015 | 3,943 | 3,984 | 3,981 | 3,982 |
| Plasma partnership | 34,879 | 35,064 | 35,417 | 35,779 | 35,861 | n/d |

**FACT.** LSIP does **not** publish a hectare-by-age-bracket table in the FY2025 annual report,
the FY2025 sustainability report, or the quarterly investor highlights. The words "age profile"
and "average age" do not appear. *Insufficient direct evidence — the age distribution must be
inferred.* The inference below is, however, tightly constrained by three disclosed series.

### Inference 1 — the replanting rate is roughly half of what the estate requires

**FACT.** Immature oil palm = 6,054 ha at 31-Dec-2025 = **6.7%** of the 90,798 ha oil-palm area.
**FACT (AR2025 accounting policy).** Immature bearer plants are reclassified to mature when
available for harvest — industry-standard ~3 years.

**INFERENCE.** Immature stock ÷ 3 years ⇒ implied new/replanting rate ≈ **2,018 ha per year**.
Against 90,798 ha of oil palm that is an implied replanting cycle of **≈ 45 years**.
A commercially normal oil-palm cycle is 25 years (some operators stretch to 28); steady state
for a 25-year cycle with 3 years of immaturity is ~12% of area immature and ~3,630 ha/yr replanted.
**LSIP is replanting at roughly 55% of the rate its own stated area implies it needs.**

**INFERENCE.** The immature share has fallen every year — 8,223 → 7,409 → 6,561 → 6,211 → 6,054 ha.
This is not a company completing a replanting programme and about to enjoy a yield uplift;
it is a company **replanting less each year**. The immature-hectare decline is the arithmetic
signature of deferral, not of completion.

**THESIS.** Deferred replanting flatters current cash flow twice: capex is lower today, and the
area that should be sitting immature is instead still being harvested at declining yields.
LSIP's FY2025 capex of Rp433bn against Rp373bn of COGS depreciation and amortisation
(AR2025 Note 22) is consistent with an estate being maintained, not renewed.

### Inference 2 — the yield series says the estate is past peak

**FACT (AR2025 Operational Highlights).** Nucleus FFB production ('000 t): 1,204 (2021) → 1,174 (2022)
→ 1,177 (2023) → 1,173 (2024) → **1,139 (2025)**. **Five consecutive years without growth; 2025 was
a 3% decline.** 1H2026 nucleus FFB was 505kt, flat yoy (1H26 Highlights, 31-Jul-2026).

**INFERENCE (nucleus FFB ÷ mature ha):**

| | 2021 | 2022 | 2023 | 2024 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| FFB yield, t/ha | 14.1 | 14.0 | 13.8 | 13.8 | **13.4** |

**FACT / INFERENCE (peer benchmark).** Indonesian large-estate peers report materially higher
nucleus yields — AALI and TAPG in the ~24 t/ha range and DSNG ~22 t/ha in recent broker and
company disclosure. LSIP's 13.4 t/ha sits at roughly **55-60% of best-in-class peer yield** and
around the level of the Indonesian *smallholder-inclusive national average*, not the large-estate average.

**INFERENCE.** A 13.4 t/ha yield on flat mature hectarage, falling for five years, with only
6.7% of the area immature, is the classic signature of a **materially over-aged estate**. Well-managed
North/South Sumatra estates on prime-age palms (9-18 years) do 20-25 t/ha; 13.4 t/ha is what
palms past ~22-25 years produce, and is also what happens when replanting has been deferred long
enough that the old-palm cohort dominates the mix.

**INFERENCE (share past peak).** Working backwards: to depress a whole-estate average to 13.4 t/ha,
a very large share of the mature area must be past its peak. *A precise figure cannot be derived
without the disclosed age table.* A reasonable bracket, given a 45-year implied cycle and 6.7%
immature, is that **on the order of 30-45% of mature hectares are 20+ years old** — but this is
an ASSUMPTION and must be flagged as such. It is not a disclosed number.

### Inference 3 — the estate is shrinking, not just ageing

**FACT.** Total nucleus planted area fell from 114,111 ha (2021) to 109,258 ha (30-Jun-2026),
a loss of **4,853 ha (-4.3%)** in four and a half years. Oil palm alone fell 93,853 → 89,559 ha (-4.6%).
Rubber fell 16,228 → 15,717 ha in 1H26 alone.

**INFERENCE.** LSIP is not merely under-replanting — hectares are leaving the balance sheet
altogether. The AR2025 does not explain the shrinkage. *Insufficient evidence on cause —
requires further research* (candidate explanations: HGU land-right expiries, land releases to
plasma/community, or estates simply abandoned rather than replanted).

**THESIS.** On disclosed evidence, the productive base of this company is contracting on three
axes simultaneously: hectares (-4.6%), yield per hectare (-5% over 5 years), and total nucleus
FFB (-5% over 5 years). Every rupiah of the earnings growth since 2023 is price, not volume.

### Priority-1 conclusion

**THESIS.** LSIP's tree-age problem is real, quantifiable from disclosed data, and unaddressed.
The FY2025 result — record profit on the lowest FFB production in five years — is a
**price event, not an operating improvement**. Because replanting is running at roughly half the
required rate, the yield decline is not a cycle that self-corrects: it compounds. Absent a step-up
in replanting capex (which would depress cash flow for 3-4 years before helping), the base case
for nucleus FFB over 2026-2030 is **flat to further declining**, not recovering.

## PRIORITY 2 — GOVERNANCE

### The ownership chain

**FACT (AR2025 shareholder structure, p.12; IndoAgri disclosure).**

- PT Salim Ivomas Pratama Tbk (**SIMP**, IDX-listed) holds **59.51%** of LSIP (4,058,425,010 shares).
- Indofood Agri Resources Ltd (**IndoAgri**, SGX:5JS) holds a further **0.11%** directly, and owns ~73.46% of SIMP.
- PT Indofood Sukses Makmur Tbk (**ISM**) held **85.90%** of IndoAgri at 31-Dec-2025.
- Mr Anthoni Salim's total interest in IndoAgri was **86.04%** at 31-Dec-2025, via First Pacific (HKEX) and Indofood.
- **Public float: 40.38%** (2,753,756,655 shares), all holders below 5%. ~17,900 shareholders (AR2025).

**INFERENCE.** Salim's *look-through economic* interest in LSIP is roughly
0.859 × 0.7346 × 0.5951 ≈ **37.5%**, while control is 100%. Minorities supply ~62% of the economics
and hold 0% of the control. This is a four-layer pyramid — the classic structure in which
control and cash-flow rights diverge, and the classic setting for value transfer.

**FACT.** The board is fully Salim/Indofood-interlocked: the President Commissioner and multiple
commissioners and directors hold concurrent SIMP, IndoAgri, Indofood and First Pacific roles
(AR2025 board biographies). Commissioner Axton Salim sits on the board. The AGM is held at
Indofood Tower.

### Related-party CPO sales to the parent

**FACT (AR2025 Note 27 and Note on customer concentration).** Sales to SIMP, the direct parent:

| | 2025 | 2024 |
|---|---|---|
| Sales to SIMP (Rp m) | 3,486,712 | 3,640,887 |
| % of consolidated sales | **63.26%** | **79.80%** |
| Total related-party revenue | 3,554,637 (64.49%) | 3,643,762 (79.86%) |

**FACT.** AR2025 states all SIMP sales "were based on arm's length commercial terms" and that
related-party prices are "agreed prices … with reference to market prices."

**INFERENCE.** There is no independent verification of transfer pricing. LSIP does not disclose
realised CPO ASP per tonne alongside the KPB benchmark in a form that would let a minority
shareholder test the claim. One data point is suggestive rather than conclusive: in FY2025 LSIP's
palm-product revenue rose 21% while CPO volume rose 4% and PK volume 12% — an implied ASP rise
of roughly 12-15% against a domestic KPB benchmark that rose only **8%** (AR2025). That is
directionally *favourable* to LSIP, which argues against systematic under-pricing in 2025.
**THESIS:** the transfer-price risk is real and structural but is not, on this evidence,
demonstrably being exercised against minorities right now. It remains an uncapped, unauditable risk.

**FACT (structural risk marker).** The SIMP trade receivable swung from Rp466.7bn (3.37% of assets)
at end-2024 to **nil** at end-2025, replaced by a Rp163.6bn **contract liability owed to SIMP**
(10.60% of total liabilities) — i.e. SIMP prepaying. Working-capital terms with the parent are
evidently set bilaterally and move in size year to year.

### The cash pile — and where it is parked

**FACT.** Cash and equivalents: Rp3,847bn (2022) → 4,511 (2023) → 5,454 (2024) → **7,597 (2025)**
→ **8,372bn (30-Jun-2026)**. Total debt: **zero**. Net cash of Rp8.37tn against a market
capitalisation of ~Rp9.99tn at IDR 1,465 — **cash alone is ~84% of the market value**,
and enterprise value is ~Rp1.6tn against FY2025 operating profit of Rp2.01tn.

**FACT (AR2025 Note 27 — this is the finding that matters).** **Rp1,639,556m (Rp1.64 trillion)
of LSIP's cash — 10.55% of total assets, and ~20% of the entire cash pile — is deposited at
PT Bank Ina Perdana Tbk, disclosed as an "Other Related Party."** This was Rp906,672m (6.55% of
assets) in 2024 — the related-party deposit **nearly doubled in one year**. Finance income from
Bank Ina Perdana was Rp44,005m, **15.94% of LSIP's total finance income**.

**INFERENCE / THESIS.** This is the single hardest governance fact in the file. Minority
shareholders' undistributed cash is materially funding the deposit base of a Salim-affiliated
bank, at a scale that grew 81% year-on-year, while the payout ratio was simultaneously *cut*.
Whether or not the deposit rate is market, the arrangement means the "fortress balance sheet"
is not neutral, idle, ring-fenced cash — it is a related-party exposure and a source of
funding benefit to the controlling group. Any valuation that adds back Rp8.37tn of cash at 100%
of face value is implicitly assuming minorities can access it. They cannot.

### Distribution policy

**FACT (AR2025).** FY2024 dividend: Rp443.3bn, **Rp65/share, ~30% payout**, paid 18-Jul-2025.
FY2023 dividend: Rp266.0bn, Rp39/share, **~35% payout**, paid 25-Jul-2024. The payout ratio
was **reduced** from 35% to 30% in a year when profit rose 94% and cash rose 39%.

**FACT.** No buyback programme. The only treasury-share activity disclosed in the corporate
history is 2.9m shares bought and subsequently withdrawn — immaterial. The 25-Jun-2026 AGM agenda
contains the routine "determination of the use of net profit"; no special dividend, capital return,
or capital-reduction item.

**INFERENCE.** Retained cash is being neither distributed, nor reinvested in replanting (see
Priority 1), nor deployed in acquisitions. Capital-expenditure commitments at 31-Dec-2025 were
Rp71.8bn plus USD216,681 — trivial against Rp8.4tn of cash. The cash is simply accumulating.
**THESIS: the market's 30%+ discount to book and 0.7x EV/EBIT are not a mispricing of the
operations — they are a rational discount applied to cash the minority cannot reach and does not
control, held by a controller with an interest in the share price staying low.**

### Take-private / squeeze-out history and prospects

**FACT.** In April 2019 PT Indofood Sukses Makmur made a voluntary offer for **IndoAgri** (the
SGX-listed layer) at S$0.28, later revised to S$0.3275 ex-dividend, intending to delist it.
The offer **did not succeed** and IndoAgri remains SGX-listed.

**FACT.** Analyst commentary published in March 2026 speculates on a renewed ISM privatisation
of IndoAgri, estimating a minimum offer around S$0.525 for ~S$102m of free float. **No formal
offer has been announced.**

**INFERENCE.** Two things must be kept separate. (a) A privatisation of *IndoAgri* is an SGX
transaction that changes nothing for LSIP minorities — LSIP would still be 59.51% SIMP-owned and
IDX-listed. (b) A take-private of *LSIP itself* would require buying out a 40.38% float worth
~Rp4.0tn at the current price — funded, note, substantially by LSIP's own Rp8.4tn cash pile once
control is consolidated. **THESIS: the existence of Rp8.4tn of trapped cash inside a
59.5%-controlled subsidiary trading at 0.7x book is precisely the setup that makes a low-ball
tender attractive to the controller.** The 2019 precedent shows this group does attempt
take-privates and does start low.

**INFERENCE (the asymmetry minorities face).** If a tender comes, it is likely to be pitched off
the depressed market price, not off net cash plus estate value. Indonesian rules give no
guaranteed appraisal remedy comparable to a US-style dissenters' right. The optionality here
cuts **against** the minority as often as for it: a tender is the most plausible catalyst for
the discount closing, and also the most plausible mechanism for the discount being crystallised
permanently at a level well below intrinsic value.

**Verdict on governance: a persistent, structural, quantified discount driver — not a
resolvable one.** The specific, dated, disclosed items (63% parent sales; Rp1.64tn at a
related-party bank, doubled in a year; payout cut from 35% to 30%; a 2019 low-ball take-private
attempt at the parent) are collectively enough to justify a permanent haircut to any cash-inclusive
valuation. **RECOMMENDATION TO THE VALUATION AGENT: do not credit the cash at 100%.**

## OPERATIONS

**Yield per hectare vs Indonesian average.** FACT/INFERENCE as above: 13.4 t/ha FFB (nucleus,
2025), versus large-estate peers at ~20-24 t/ha. LSIP is a **bottom-quartile yielder among listed
Indonesian estates**. This is the most important operating number in the file and it has fallen
every year since 2021.

**Extraction rate (OER).** FACT: CPO OER 21.6% (9M24) → 21.8% (9M25) → **22.4% (1H26)**;
FY2025 CPO 292kt on 1,343kt FFB = 21.7%. PK extraction 6.0-6.3%. **INFERENCE:** OER is
respectable and *improving* — this is the one operating metric moving the right way, and it is
the reason CPO output rose 2% in 2025 despite FFB falling 3%. Industry good practice is ~22-23%;
LSIP is now within it. **Milling is competently run. The estate is not.**

**Own-estate vs third-party fruit.** FACT: FY2025 total FFB 1,343kt of which nucleus 1,139kt and
**external 204kt (15.2%)**; 1H26 external 105kt of 610kt (17.2%). FFB purchase cost rose to
**Rp672.3bn in 2025 from Rp488.9bn in 2024 (+37.5%)** (AR2025 Note 22). **INFERENCE:** LSIP is
buying an increasing share of its throughput on the open market at market prices to keep mills
loaded — margin-dilutive, and a direct consequence of the falling own-estate yield. This is a
strategic weakening, not a growth initiative. **Plasma partnership area is 35,861 ha** and grew
slightly, providing a partially captive external supply channel.

**Mill capacity and utilisation.** FACT: 12 palm oil mills, total annual FFB capacity
**2.7 million tonnes** (AR2025). Actual FY2025 throughput ~1,343kt. **INFERENCE: ~50% capacity
utilisation.** Half the milling asset base is idle. This is very large stranded capacity, and it
carries fixed cost and depreciation. It is simultaneously an *asset* (headroom to absorb any
future FFB recovery or third-party fruit at near-zero incremental capital) and a *liability*
(evidence of how far production has fallen relative to the footprint the mills were built for).

**Cost structure.** FACT (AR2025 Note 22, Rp m): indirect cost allocation 746,204;
FFB purchases 672,332; harvesting 604,326; upkeep and fertiliser 531,278; depreciation and
amortisation 373,058; manufacturing 247,715 — **total manufacturing cost Rp3,174,913m** (+12.4% yoy).
Cost of goods sold rose **26%** in 2025 against 21% revenue growth; gross margin fell 44% → 41%.
**In 1H2026 gross margin collapsed to 32% from 40% and gross profit fell 7% yoy on 16% higher sales.**
**INFERENCE: cost inflation is now outrunning price.** Core profit in 1H26 rose only 2% yoy
(Rp806bn vs Rp791bn) versus reported attributable profit +25% — the reported number is flattered
by non-core items. This is the most important recent datapoint and it is negative.

**Rubber and non-palm.** FACT: 16,203 ha rubber (15,717 ha at 1H26), of which 14,878 ha mature,
producing **~4,500 t in 2025 (4,800 t in 2024)**. Three crumb-rubber lines (26,000 t capacity)
and two sheet lines (7,000 t). Cocoa: 2,821 ha producing **475 t** (753 t in 2024). Tea: 866 ha,
1,089 t, sold under Kahuripan and Lonsum 1908 brands. Seeds: SumBio R&D facility, Bah Lias,
sold **7.8 million oil-palm seeds** externally in 2025.

**INFERENCE.** Rubber yield is **~0.30 t/ha** on mature area against a normal Indonesian estate
figure of 1.0-1.5 t/ha, and the rubber processing lines are running at ~14% of capacity
(4,500 t against 33,000 t). Cocoa is ~0.17 t/ha. **These are not underperforming assets; they
are effectively non-operating.** 15% of the planted footprint (rubber) generates ~3% of sales.
**THESIS: rubber and cocoa are, on current evidence, land held rather than businesses run —
a latent conversion-to-palm or disposal opportunity that management has not taken, and another
instance of capital sitting idle.** The seed business (SumBio) is the genuine exception: a real,
differentiated, decades-old breeding asset with external customers, and the only part of the
company with a defensible intangible.

## INDUSTRY

**Global CPO supply and demand.** FACT: CPO traded RM4,400-4,650/t in August 2026 per MPOC
guidance, supported by Indonesian biodiesel demand and firm energy prices. Malaysian June 2026
output rose 8% m/m to 1.63mt with stocks at 2.5mt. GAPKI reported Indonesian 9M2025 production
of 39.60mt, +11% yoy. Indonesian CPO output is forecast to rise from ~48mt (2025) to ~50mt (2026),
leaving ~22mt of exports even under a B50 scenario. FACT: LSIP's realised domestic KPB CPO price
averaged **Rp14,234/kg in 2025, +8%**, while CIF Rotterdam rose 15% to USD1,283/t.

**INFERENCE.** The 7-point gap between the international price rise (+15%) and the domestic
price rise (+8%) is the export levy and DMO working exactly as designed: **the state captures a
meaningful share of any CPO price upcycle before it reaches the grower.** This is the single most
important industry fact for an Indonesian upstream producer, and it caps the earnings leverage
LSIP offers to a CPO rally.

**Export levy and DMO.** FACT: the CPO export levy was raised by 2.5 percentage points from
March 2026 (reported as moving to 12.5%) specifically to fund the biodiesel mandate. DMO
obligations for cooking oil remain in force. **INFERENCE:** the levy is structurally a
grower-funded subsidy to the domestic biodiesel programme; the higher the mandate, the higher
the levy needed, so **B50 upside and levy drag are the same policy** and partially cancel for an
upstream pure-play like LSIP. A downstream-integrated group (SIMP/IndoAgri) captures the biodiesel
and refining margin; **LSIP, sitting upstream and selling 63% of its output to that group,
does not.**

**B40 / B50 biodiesel mandate.** FACT: B40 ran through 2025 (14.2bn litres of biodiesel).
B50 was repeatedly delayed — scrapped for early 2026 in January, with B45 floated as an interim
step in late 2025 — and was announced by the Energy Minister on 6-Apr-2026 for rollout from
**1 July 2026**, requiring ~20bn litres/yr and ~3.5mt of additional CPO. **INFERENCE:** B50 is
genuinely demand-positive for CPO prices and is the main reason the price is holding above
RM4,400. But its history is one of repeated slippage on cost grounds (the palm/gasoil spread
above USD350/t makes it expensive to fund), and its funding mechanism is the levy on LSIP's
own sales. **ASSUMPTION for the valuation agent: do not underwrite B50 as a durable earnings
tailwind for an upstream producer; underwrite it as price support with an offsetting fiscal cost.**

**Soy substitution.** FACT: US soybean oil has carried an unusually high premium since March 2026,
with US soyoil exports down 69% in the first five months of 2026; combined soybean/sunflower/rapeseed
output growth for 2026-27 is forecast at +16.5mt versus a four-year average of +22.7mt.
**INFERENCE:** the substitute complex is tight and expensive, which is supportive for palm demand
in the near term. This is a cyclical, not structural, positive and is fully in the current price.

**Land expansion constraints.** FACT: LSIP's own planted area has *shrunk* 4.3% since 2021 and
capex commitments are negligible. **INFERENCE:** consistent with the well-documented Indonesian
reality that new HGU concessions and forest-release permits are effectively unobtainable for
large corporates. **THESIS: this is genuinely double-edged. It makes LSIP's existing 89,559 ha
of planted oil palm a scarce, non-replicable asset (supports the asset-value case), and it
simultaneously means the only route to growth is yield — which requires the replanting LSIP is
not doing.**

**RSPO and EUDR.** FACT: LSIP is **ISPO**-certified — 38 of 38 estates ISPO-certified or audited,
12 of 12 mills ISPO or PROPER certified, and the great majority of nucleus CPO production is
ISPO-certified. **FACT: the word "RSPO" appears zero times in either the FY2025 Annual Report or
the FY2025 Sustainability Report.** The company holds NDPE-style commitments (no deforestation,
no peat, no exploitation) and applies TCFD, but not RSPO certification. FACT: EUDR now applies
from **30 December 2026** for large and medium operators (June 2027 for micro/small) after two
one-year delays; GAPKI is lobbying for smallholder exemptions and for ISPO to be recognised.

**INFERENCE.** The absence of RSPO is a real, if second-order, commercial limitation on direct
EU access. In LSIP's specific case it matters less than it would for a peer, because **63% of
output goes to the parent's domestic refining system rather than to EU buyers** — LSIP has
effectively outsourced its market-access problem to SIMP. That is a mitigant to EUDR risk and
simultaneously another expression of the company's dependence on its controller.

**Competitive Advantage:** Weak and narrowing. The durable elements are (i) an irreplaceable
land and permit position in a country where new concessions are not available; (ii) the SumBio
seed-breeding operation, a genuine multi-decade intangible with external customers; and (iii) a
captive offtaker that removes all marketing, logistics and credit risk (18-day collection period,
DSO effectively 1 day at 2Q26). None of these is a cost advantage. **LSIP's unit economics are
worse than its peers' because its trees are older, and no amount of milling efficiency (OER 22.4%,
genuinely good) offsets a 13.4 t/ha field yield against peers at 20-24 t/ha.** In a commodity
business, being a high-cost-per-tonne producer is the opposite of a moat.

**Management:** Competent stewards, not operators. The evidence is consistent: mills are run well
(OER rising), the balance sheet is immaculate (zero debt, current ratio 9.45x), costs are
*reported* as a priority, and disclosure is clean and on time. But the core agronomic decision —
replanting — has been deferred for at least five years, non-palm assets producing near-nothing
have been left in place for years, and half the milling capacity sits idle. Management is fully
interlocked with the controlling group; the board contains no evident independent voice
championing minority interests. **INFERENCE: this is a management team optimising the parent's
consolidated position, not LSIP's standalone per-share value.**

**Capital Allocation:** Poor, and the clearest single argument against the stock. Rp8.37tn of
cash — 84% of market capitalisation — earns deposit interest (of which ~16% of finance income
comes from a related-party bank holding Rp1.64tn of it) while the operating asset that generates
the cash decays for want of replanting capex. Payout was *cut* from 35% to 30% as cash rose 39%.
No buyback exists despite the stock at 0.70x book. Capex of Rp433bn barely exceeds depreciation.
**Every available lever — replant, distribute, repurchase, redeploy the idle rubber land — is
unpulled. The capital-allocation failure is not an oversight; it is the strategy.**

**Industry Outlook:** Constructive prices, capped grower economics. CPO is well supported into
2027 by B50 (from 1-Jul-2026), a tight substitute complex, and constrained land supply. But the
Indonesian policy architecture is explicitly designed to transfer the upcycle from the upstream
grower to the biodiesel programme via the export levy — visibly so in 2025 (domestic +8% vs
Rotterdam +15%) and again with the March 2026 levy increase. EUDR from 30-Dec-2026 raises
compliance cost sector-wide; LSIP's ISPO-only, no-RSPO position is a mild relative negative
mitigated by its domestic sales channel. For an upstream, non-integrated, low-yield producer,
the industry offers **support, not leverage**.

**Industry: NEUTRAL** — strong near-term price support (B50, tight substitutes, no new land)
fully offset by the levy/DMO mechanism that caps how much of it reaches an upstream grower,
plus rising EUDR compliance cost.

**Company: HEADWIND** — five straight years of flat-to-declining nucleus FFB, yield down to
13.4 t/ha, planted area shrinking 4.3%, replanting at ~55% of requirement, gross margin down
44%→41%→32% (1H26), core profit +2% yoy in 1H26, and a growing reliance on purchased third-party
fruit to keep half-empty mills loaded.

**Structural Advantages:**
1. **Fortress, debt-free balance sheet.** Zero total debt, Rp8.37tn net cash (84% of market cap),
   current ratio 9.45x, interest coverage effectively infinite. Survival risk is nil; the company
   can fund a full replanting programme out of cash without touching a bank.
2. **Irreplaceable land and permit bank plus surplus milling capacity.** 89,559 ha of planted
   oil palm, 35,861 ha of plasma, and 12 mills with 2.7mt/yr capacity running at ~50% — in a
   country where new HGU is effectively unobtainable. Any yield recovery is absorbed at near-zero
   incremental capital.
3. **SumBio seed business and integrated ISPO-certified operations with a captive offtaker.**
   7.8m seeds sold externally in 2025, 38/38 estates and 12/12 mills ISPO-certified, and a parent
   that takes 63% of output — eliminating marketing, logistics and credit risk (DSO 18 days).

**Structural Problems:**
1. **An ageing, under-replanted, shrinking estate.** 6.7% immature implies a ~45-year replanting
   cycle against a 25-year commercial life; nucleus FFB has not grown since 2021 and yield has
   fallen to 13.4 t/ha versus peers at 20-24 t/ha. This is a self-compounding decline, not a cycle.
2. **Minority shareholders control neither the cash nor the customer.** SIMP owns 59.51% and buys
   63% of output; Rp1.64tn of LSIP's cash (10.55% of assets, up 81% yoy) sits at a related-party
   bank; payout was cut from 35% to 30% while cash rose 39%; no buyback at 0.70x book. Salim's
   look-through economics are ~37.5% against 100% control, and the group attempted a low-ball
   take-private of the parent listing in 2019.
3. **Structurally idle and misallocated assets.** ~50% mill utilisation; 16,203 ha of rubber
   yielding ~0.30 t/ha (versus a 1.0-1.5 t/ha norm) into processing lines at ~14% utilisation;
   2,821 ha of cocoa yielding ~0.17 t/ha. Roughly 18% of the planted footprint produces ~5% of sales.

## KEY QUESTION — good business mispriced, mediocre business cheap, or structurally cheap for a reason?

**THESIS: structurally cheap for a reason, and the reasons are two, both durable.**

The first reason is operational. LSIP is a **below-median operator of a commodity asset** —
13.4 t/ha against peers at 20-24 t/ha, declining for five years, with replanting at half the
required rate and the planted footprint shrinking. Record 2025 profit came entirely from price;
1H26 already shows the reversal (gross margin 40%→32%, core profit +2%). A screener sees ROE 12.9%
and F-Score 7 and calls this a compounder. It is the opposite: a slowly liquidating estate whose
current earnings are being flattered by the very underinvestment that guarantees they will not last.

The second reason is structural ownership. The headline cheapness — 0.7x EV/EBIT, 0.70x book,
cash at 84% of market cap — is almost entirely an artefact of a cash pile the minority cannot
reach, held inside a four-layer pyramid by a controller with ~37.5% of the economics and 100% of
the control, 20% of it deposited at that controller's own bank, with payout cut and no buyback.
**The discount has persisted for a decade** (5-year price CAGR 0.98%; the stock sits at the 69th
percentile of its own 10-year range while cash has more than doubled since 2022). A discount that
survives a doubling of net cash is not a mispricing awaiting recognition; it is the market
correctly pricing the probability that this cash is never distributed to minorities.

**What would change the verdict** (falsifiable, and worth Stage-2 monitoring):
a step-change in immature hectares above ~10,000 ha (real replanting has started); a payout ratio
above 60% or a buyback authorisation; reduction of the Bank Ina Perdana deposit; or a tender offer
priced with reference to net cash plus estate value rather than to the depressed market price.
None of these is currently in evidence.

**What makes it not a zero:** the assets are real and unencumbered, the company cannot go bust,
it pays a ~5.7% dividend, and the surplus mill capacity plus non-replicable land bank mean that
*if* replanting were funded from the existing cash, the operating recovery would be substantial
and would need no external capital. The optionality exists. The controller has shown no interest
in exercising it.

**Business Verdict: ASSET VALUE — with high VALUE TRAP risk.**

*Rationale for the dual label: the asset backing is genuine and verified (89,559 ha planted oil
palm, 12 mills, Rp8.37tn net cash, zero debt), which distinguishes this from a pure value trap
where the assets themselves are impaired. But the mechanism by which that value reaches a minority
shareholder is controlled entirely by a related party that has, on five years of disclosed
evidence, chosen not to operate it. Underwrite this as an asset-value situation in which the
central question is not "what is it worth" but "who gets it" — and apply a permanent haircut to
the cash. It is emphatically not a compounder, and it is not a clean cyclical either, because
the volume base is structurally declining rather than cycling.*
